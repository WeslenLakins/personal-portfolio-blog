Lawyers have gotten comfortable storing confidential client files in the cloud. OneDrive, Google Drive, Dropbox, practice-management vaults—ordinary tools. Ethics guidance has largely treated that as fine when the lawyer uses reasonable care: pick the provider carefully, understand the security model, limit access, and supervise the vendor.

Consumer chatbots landed differently. Paste a client name, a deal term, a litigation theory, or a draft email into a public generative AI tool, and the room goes quiet. The instinct is often: *that might breach confidentiality—or worse, privilege.*

Here is the tension. With a chatbot, you might share only a fragment of the matter. With a cloud drive, you might upload the entire file. Yet the ethics and privilege conversation often panics more about the chat than the drive. Why?

Not because cloud storage is magically “inside” the attorney-client relationship and chatbots are not. Both typically involve a third-party system. The better answer is that **confidentiality ethics** and **privilege doctrine** ask different questions—and consumer generative AI often fails tests that carefully used cloud storage has been built to pass.

## Confidentiality Is Not the Same as Privilege

**Confidentiality** is an ethics duty. Under the ABA Model Rules, a lawyer generally may not reveal information relating to the representation of a client unless the client gives informed consent, the disclosure is impliedly authorized to carry out the representation, or an exception applies. Lawyers must also make “reasonable efforts to prevent the inadvertent or unauthorized disclosure of, or unauthorized access to,” that information. ABA Model Rules of Prof’l Conduct r. 1.6(a), (c) (2023). Tennessee’s Rule 1.6 imposes parallel confidentiality obligations. See Tenn. R. Prof’l Cond. 1.6; Bd. of Prof’l Responsibility of the Supreme Court of Tenn., Formal Ethics Op. 2015-F-159 (Sept. 11, 2015).

That duty is broad. It covers more than privileged communications. It is about professional obligation, not just courtroom admissibility.

**Attorney-client privilege** and **work-product** are evidentiary shields. In federal court, privilege generally protects communications (1) between client and counsel (2) that were intended to be, and were, kept confidential (3) for the purpose of obtaining or providing legal advice. See, e.g., United States v. Mejia, 655 F.3d 126, 132 (2d Cir. 2011). Work-product protects materials prepared in anticipation of litigation, with special protection for mental impressions.

You can violate confidentiality ethics without anyone litigating waiver—and a privilege fight can turn on facts the ethics rules only partly address. When people say “putting this in ChatGPT waives privilege,” they are often mixing the two. Both matter. They are not interchangeable.

## Cloud Storage: The “Reasonable Care” Path

Tennessee’s Board of Professional Responsibility has long said a lawyer may store confidential client information in the cloud if the lawyer takes reasonable care to keep it confidential and protect it from breach, loss, and related risks. The Board emphasizes competence in selecting and continuing to use the provider, plus reasonable efforts to ensure the vendor’s conduct is compatible with the lawyer’s obligations under Rules 1.1, 1.6, 1.9(c), and 5.3. Formal Ethics Op. 2015-F-159.

ABA Formal Opinion 477R points the same direction: the Model Rules do not invent a special confidentiality duty for each technology. What changes is the reasonableness analysis—threat model, transmission and storage practices, security measures, vendor diligence, and when sensitivity calls for more protection. ABA Comm. on Ethics & Prof’l Responsibility, Formal Op. 477R (2017). The opinion expressly contemplates well-vetted cloud file storage for exchanging sensitive documents.

In practice, lawyers treat a business-grade cloud drive as a **system of record**—access-controlled storage so authorized people can do the work. Diligence focuses on security, access, retention, breach response, and contractual confidentiality—not on whether the platform will reuse your deposition to train a public model. Uploading a complete confidential PDF to OneDrive does not, standing alone, equal “breaching privilege.” The ethics question is reasonable care; the privilege question is whether confidentiality held. Neither is answered by “cloud = waiver”—or by familiarity. Weak sharing links, no MFA, or an unvetted repository can still be a problem.

## Generative AI: Same Duties, Sharper Disclosure Mechanics

In July 2024, the ABA issued Formal Opinion 512—the first formal opinion on lawyers’ use of generative AI—applying familiar rules on competence, confidentiality, communication, supervision, candor, and fees. Lawyers must understand how a tool handles inputs before putting information relating to a representation into it. For many self-learning tools, **informed client consent is generally required before inputting that information**; boilerplate engagement-letter “AI use” language is not enough. ABA Comm. on Ethics & Prof’l Responsibility, Formal Op. 512 (July 29, 2024).

Tennessee has not issued a generative-AI-specific formal ethics opinion. Existing Tennessee rules on competence, confidentiality, and supervision still apply, and Opinion 512 is the leading national roadmap. See Tenn. R. Prof’l Cond. 1.1, 1.6, 5.3; Formal Ethics Op. 2015-F-159. The ethics story is not “chatbots forbidden, drives allowed.” It is: **know what happens to the data, match the tool to the risk, and get real informed consent when the tool’s design creates disclosure risk.**

## Why Volume Is a Red Herring

Privilege and confidentiality do not grade risk by megabytes. What often differs with **consumer / public chatbots** is:

1. **Use of inputs** — retention, model improvement, or secondary use of prompts and outputs, unlike access-controlled storage.
2. **Reasonable expectation of confidentiality** — terms that warn of secondary use or third-party (including government) disclosure can undercut what privilege requires.
3. **Advice-seeking form** — courts may treat a chat as a disclosure outside the privileged relationship, especially when no lawyer directed the use.
4. **Discoverability** — prompts and outputs can become seizable, subpoenaable records separate from the file in a vault.

A well-configured cloud drive is usually **infrastructure for the representation**, not a conversational third party. Storage already has a diligence grammar that consumer chat products often fail on day one. Cloud feels administrative; chatbots feel like thinking out loud. Usefulness is exactly when confidentiality discipline matters most.

## Privilege: Where the Third-Party Problem Gets Sharp

Ethics confidentiality can be satisfied or violated without a privilege motion. Privilege fights ask whether a communication was confidential and within the privileged relationship—or whether a third-party disclosure destroyed the protection.

In *United States v. Heppner*, No. 25 Cr. 503 (JSR) (S.D.N.Y. Feb. 17, 2026) (Rakoff, J.), the court held that a criminal defendant’s written exchanges with the consumer version of Claude were protected by neither attorney-client privilege nor the work-product doctrine. Treating the issue as one of first impression, the court reasoned that Claude is not an attorney; that the platform’s privacy policy—collecting inputs and outputs, training on that data, and reserving disclosure rights to third parties including governmental authorities—defeated any reasonable expectation of confidentiality; and that the defendant used the tool on his own, not at counsel’s direction.

One decision does not settle every jurisdiction or counsel-directed enterprise workflow. It does illustrate the point: **the chatbot is not “your associate,” and consumer terms may destroy the confidentiality story privilege requires.** “I only pasted a little” is cold comfort—these analyses care about the nature of the disclosure and the expectation of privacy, not file size.

## What Actually Matters

**Cloud drive:** storage infrastructure; reasonable care and vendor diligence (Formal Ethics Op. 2015-F-159; Formal Op. 477R); privilege turns on whether confidentiality held with reasonable controls.

**Consumer chatbot:** generative processor, often with secondary use of inputs; Opinion 512’s consent and diligence emphasis; privilege turns on whether the chat was a confidential communication in a privileged relationship—or a disclosure to a third-party system.

**Volume** is often high on the drive and low in the chat—and still not the test.

Checklist: terms and data use (training, retention, disclosure language, zero-retention options); expectation of confidentiality; counsel direction vs. solo public-chat use; specific informed consent when Opinion 512 requires it (not boilerplate); vendor diligence and supervision under Tenn. R. Prof’l Cond. 5.3 and Formal Op. 477R; minimization; and jurisdiction—Tennessee Rules and TBPR guidance first, ABA opinions as persuasive authority. See Tenn. R. Prof’l Cond. 1.1, 1.6; Formal Ethics Op. 2015-F-159; Formal Ops. 477R & 512.

## A Practical Working Rule

Use cloud systems of record the way ethics opinions already contemplate: with competence and reasonable safeguards—not on vibes.

Use generative AI the way Opinion 512 contemplates: understand the tool, prefer configurations that do not learn from your matters, keep client information out of consumer self-learning products unless you have a clear diligence story and, where required, informed consent, and never outsource legal judgment or citation-checking to a model.

When someone says “but we put the whole file in Drive,” answer the real question: **Did that system preserve confidentiality under controls we can defend—or did we disclose matter information into a product that treats our prompt like raw material?** Those are not the same act with different file sizes. They are different risk designs.

The tools are useful. The ethics rules are workable. The privilege issues are getting less theoretical. The lawyers who stay out of trouble are not the ones who ban every new product. They are the ones who stop confusing *familiar* with *safe*—and *scary* with *unique*.
