Lawyers have gotten comfortable storing confidential client files in the cloud. Microsoft OneDrive, Google Drive, Dropbox, practice-management vaults—these are ordinary parts of practice. Ethics guidance has largely treated that as permissible when the lawyer uses reasonable care: pick a provider carefully, understand the security model, limit access, supervise the vendor relationship, and keep going.

Consumer chatbots landed differently. Paste a client name, a deal term, a litigation theory, or a draft email into a public generative AI tool, and the room goes quiet. The instinct is often: *that might breach confidentiality—or worse, privilege.*

Here is the tension worth unpacking. With a chatbot, you might share only a fragment of the matter. With a cloud drive, you might upload the entire file. Yet the ethics and privilege conversation often panics more about the chat than the drive. Why?

The short answer is not that cloud storage is magically “inside” the attorney-client relationship and chatbots are not. Both typically involve a third-party system. The better answer is that **confidentiality ethics** and **privilege doctrine** care about different things—and consumer generative AI often fails the tests that cloud storage, used carefully, has been built to pass.

# Confidentiality Is Not the Same as Privilege

Start with the distinction that keeps conversations honest.

**Confidentiality** is an ethics duty. Under the ABA Model Rules (and Tennessee’s parallel rules), a lawyer generally may not reveal information relating to the representation of a client unless the client gives informed consent, the disclosure is impliedly authorized to carry out the representation, or an exception applies. Lawyers also must make reasonable efforts to prevent inadvertent or unauthorized disclosure of, or unauthorized access to, that information. [CITE: ABA Model Rule 1.6(a) & (c); Tenn. RPC 1.6(a) & (c)]

That duty is broad. It covers more than privileged communications. It is about professional obligation, not just courtroom admissibility.

**Attorney-client privilege** and **work-product** are evidentiary shields. Privilege generally protects confidential communications between lawyer and client for the purpose of legal advice. Work-product protects materials prepared in anticipation of litigation, with special protection for mental impressions. [CITE: verify controlling privilege / work-product standards in TN and relevant federal forum]

A practice can violate confidentiality ethics without anyone ever litigating a privilege waiver—and a privilege fight can turn on facts the ethics rules only partially address. When people say “putting this in ChatGPT waives privilege,” they are often mixing the two. Both matter. They are not interchangeable.

# Cloud Storage: The “Reasonable Care” Path Lawyers Already Know

Tennessee’s Board of Professional Responsibility has long said a lawyer may store confidential client information in the cloud if the lawyer takes reasonable care to keep it confidential and protect it from breach, loss, and related risks. The Board does not freeze a static technical standard; it emphasizes competence in selecting and continuing to use the provider, plus reasonable efforts to ensure the vendor’s conduct is compatible with the lawyer’s obligations. [CITE: TBPR Formal Ethics Opinion 2015-F-159; Tenn. RPC 1.1, 1.6, 1.9(c), 5.3]

ABA guidance on electronic communications points the same direction: the Model Rules do not invent a special confidentiality duty for each technology. What changes is the reasonableness analysis—threat model, transmission and storage practices, security measures, vendor diligence, training, and when heightened sensitivity calls for more protection. [CITE: ABA Formal Opinion 477R (2017)]

In practical terms, lawyers treat a business-grade cloud drive as a **system of record**: access-controlled storage that holds documents so the lawyer (and authorized people) can do the work. The vendor is a service provider. Diligence focuses on security, access limits, retention, breach response, and contractual confidentiality—not on whether the platform will reuse the contents of your deposition transcript to improve a public model for strangers.

That framing is why uploading a complete confidential PDF to OneDrive does not, standing alone, read like “breaching privilege” in ordinary professional conversation. The ethics question is whether your care was reasonable. The privilege question is whether you still maintained confidentiality consistent with the privilege’s requirements—fact-specific, and usually not answered by “cloud = waiver.”

None of that is a free pass for sloppy cloud use. Consumer accounts, weak sharing links, no MFA, no vendor review, or dumping files into a random “less reputable” repository without diligence can still be a problem. Familiarity is not diligence.

# Generative AI: Same Duties, Sharper Disclosure Mechanics

In July 2024, the ABA issued Formal Opinion 512—the first formal opinion focused on lawyers’ use of generative AI tools. It applies familiar rules: competence, confidentiality, communication, supervision, candor, and fees. On confidentiality, it is direct. Lawyers must understand how a tool handles inputs before putting information relating to a representation into it. For many self-learning tools—where inputs may be retained or used in ways that could lead to disclosure—**informed client consent is generally required before inputting that information**. Boilerplate engagement-letter language authorizing “AI use” is not enough. [CITE: ABA Formal Opinion 512 (July 29, 2024); ABA Model Rules 1.1, 1.4, 1.6, 5.1, 5.3]

Tennessee has not (as of this writing) issued a generative-AI-specific formal ethics opinion. That does not create a vacuum. The existing Tennessee rules on competence, confidentiality, and supervision still apply, and Opinion 512 is the leading national roadmap lawyers and carriers are using in the meantime. [CITE: confirm current TBPR AI guidance status; note reliance on Tenn. RPC 1.1 / 1.6 / 5.3 and persuasive value of ABA Op. 512]

So the ethics story is not “chatbots forbidden, drives allowed.” It is: **know what happens to the data, match the tool to the risk, and get real informed consent when the tool’s design creates disclosure risk.**

# Why the Chatbot Panic Is Not Irrational—Even When the Drive Holds More Data

Volume is a red herring. Privilege and confidentiality do not grade risk by megabytes.

What often differs with **consumer / public chatbots** is the combination of:

1. **Use of inputs.** Many consumer tools’ terms allow retention, model improvement, or broader secondary use of prompts and outputs. That is a different risk profile from a storage vendor that holds your file under access controls and does not treat your deposition as training fuel.
2. **Reasonable expectation of confidentiality.** Privilege and related doctrines care whether the communication was intended to be—and actually kept—confidential. Terms that warn of secondary use, disclosure to third parties, or government access understated conditions can undercut that expectation. [CITE: verify platform terms at time of use; discuss in light of privilege elements in controlling jurisdiction]
3. **The interaction looks like seeking help.** A chat thread can resemble asking a stranger for analysis of the case. Even if the “stranger” is software, courts and adversaries may analyze it as a disclosure outside the privileged relationship—especially when no lawyer directed the use.
4. **Discoverability of the artifact.** Prompts and outputs can become records—seizable, subpoenaable, producible—separate from the underlying client file sitting quietly in a vault.

By contrast, a well-configured cloud drive is usually understood as **infrastructure for the representation**, not a conversational third party. Courts and ethics opinions have spent years normalizing third-party vendors that facilitate legal work when confidentiality controls are reasonable. That does not mean every drive is safe. It means the legal culture already has a diligence grammar for storage that consumer chat products often fail on day one.

There is also a human factor. Cloud storage feels boring and administrative. Chatbots feel like thinking out loud with an oracle. People paste strategy into the oracle because it is useful—and usefulness is exactly when confidentiality discipline matters most.

# Privilege: Where the Third-Party Problem Gets Sharp

Ethics confidentiality can be satisfied (or violated) without a privilege motion ever being filed. Privilege fights ask whether a communication was confidential and within the privileged relationship—or whether disclosure to a third party waived protection.

Emerging case law is starting to treat public generative AI exchanges skeptically when a user, acting on their own, puts sensitive material into a consumer tool whose terms undermine confidentiality. In one recent federal decision, a court rejected attorney-client privilege and work-product claims over a defendant’s chats with a public AI platform, emphasizing that the communications were not with counsel, were not confidential in light of the platform’s privacy policy, and were not undertaken at counsel’s direction for legal advice. The court also noted that sharing otherwise privileged content with such a platform can waive privilege. [CITE: United States v. Heppner (S.D.N.Y.) — verify caption, date, and holding from primary opinion; secondary summaries describe Feb. 2026 written opinion on public Claude chats]

One case does not settle every jurisdiction, every enterprise configuration, or every counsel-directed workflow. It does illustrate the point the ethics panic has been circling: **the chatbot is not “your associate,” and consumer terms may destroy the confidentiality story privilege requires.**

That is also why “I only pasted a little” is cold comfort. Waiver and confidentiality analyses often care about the nature of the disclosure and the expectation of privacy—not whether you uploaded fifty pages or five sentences.

Work-product can be a separate analysis. Materials prepared by a client alone, for the client’s own purposes, without counsel’s direction, are often a weak fit for work-product protection—AI-assisted or not. Counsel-directed use on an enterprise tool with strong confidentiality terms may present a different record. [CITE: work-product standard in relevant forum; note Heppner-type reasoning on counsel direction]

# So What Actually Matters?

Strip away the hype and the comfort bias. The practical variables look like this:

| Factor | Cloud drive (typical business use) | Consumer chatbot (typical public use) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Role of vendor | Storage / collaboration infrastructure | Generative processing of prompts; often secondary use of inputs |
| Ethics frame | Reasonable care + vendor diligence | Same duties + Opinion 512’s consent / diligence emphasis |
| Consent posture | Often impliedly authorized as ordinary practice infrastructure *if* diligence is sound—still confirm engagement terms and client expectations | Informed consent often required before inputting representation-related info into self-learning tools |
| Privilege pressure point | Usually “did you keep it confidential with reasonable controls?” | “Was this a confidential communication in a privileged relationship—or a disclosure to a third-party system?” |
| Volume of data | Often high | Often low—but volume is not the test |

What matters, for lawyers and for sophisticated clients watching how their counsel works:

- **Terms of service and data use.** Training, retention, human review, subprocessors, law-enforcement / government disclosure language, and whether a business or zero-retention option exists.
- **Reasonable expectation of confidentiality.** If the product tells you inputs may be reused or disclosed, plan accordingly.
- **Purpose and direction.** Counsel-directed use inside a controlled environment is a different story from a solo paste into a public chat for curiosity’s sake.
- **Engagement letters and client consent.** Specific, informed consent—not boilerplate AI waivers—when Opinion 512’s conditions are in play. [CITE: ABA Formal Opinion 512 on informed consent]
- **Vendor diligence and supervision.** Same muscle you already use for cloud providers: security, access, breach notice, deletion, and whether the tool is fit for legal work. [CITE: TBPR 2015-F-159; ABA Op. 477R; Model Rules / Tenn. RPC 5.3]
- **Minimization.** Even on an approved tool, do not put in what you do not need.
- **Jurisdiction.** Privilege and ethics details are state- and forum-specific. Tennessee lawyers should read the Tennessee Rules and TBPR guidance first, then use ABA opinions as persuasive authority. [CITE: Tenn. RPC 1.1, 1.6; TBPR opinions as applicable]

# A Practical Working Rule

Use cloud systems of record the way ethics opinions already contemplate: with competence and reasonable safeguards—not on vibes.

Use generative AI the way Opinion 512 contemplates: understand the tool, prefer configurations that do not learn from your matters, keep client information out of consumer self-learning products unless you have a clear diligence story and, where required, informed consent, and never outsource legal judgment or citation-checking to a model.

And when someone says “but we put the whole file in Drive,” answer the real question: **Did that system preserve confidentiality under controls we can defend—or did we disclose matter information into a product that treats our prompt like raw material?** Those are not the same act with different file sizes. They are different risk designs.

The tools are useful. The ethics rules are workable. The privilege issues are getting less theoretical by the month. The lawyers who will stay out of trouble are not the ones who ban every new product. They are the ones who stop confusing *familiar* with *safe*—and *scary* with *unique*.
